The first ticket of the morning was Visitor stuck at glass — also someone held the door for me yesterday and it felt weird after. Jules's sticky under which decision? said did they badge, or did they smile?

"Physical security controls are the parts you can stub your toe on," she said when I reached for a blank card. "Fences, locks, cameras, badges, habits. Challenge the follow-in. Lock the laptop. Never treat a smile as authentication."

I opened the help desk queue.

Maya and the door that loves strangers

Maya from accounting was in the lobby with a client.

"I badged in," she said. "He was right behind me with a laptop bag and a confident wave. I held the door. Then I remembered the phishing week and felt like I had sponsored a heist."

I walked out. Glass vestibule. Two door locks in sequence that only unlock one side at a time when the system is happy — our mantrap. Today the outer leaf was propped with a rubber wedge because someone thought airflow was a security control.

"That wedge turns a mantrap into a courtesy hallway," I said. "You did piggybacking — he entered with your consent. If he had slipped in silently behind you without the wave, that is tailgating. Either way, he did not authenticate."

Maya looked stricken. "So I…?"

"You stop, let the door close, and send him to reception," I said. "Reception checks ID, writes the visitor log, prints a guest access badge, and assigns an escort. You do not improvise hospitality at the glass."

We pulled the wedge. Facilities Slack got a photo. The client waited at reception and was late to the meeting. Maya apologized to him more than she needed to. Jules added a sticky for the vestibule: held doors are not badges.

Bollards, fencing, and the building that pretends to be boring

Mid-morning, a new hire asked why our street face looked like a quiet office and not a datacenter postcard. Network walked him past the loading dock with me tagging along because the ticket subject was why can't trucks pull up to the glass?

"Bollards keep enthusiastic vehicles from interviewing the lobby," the engineer said. "Fencing and the gate are the outer delay. Temporary barricades go up when the street fair gets ideas. Lighting on the lot is not ambiance — dark corners are where cameras go blind and people get creative."

The building itself is deliberately dull from the sidewalk — no giant logo, no SERVERS INSIDE sculpture. Jules called it industrial camouflage without romance. Signage is sparse and specific: Authorized personnel, All visitors check in, not a tour brochure.

I wrote the desk line: perimeter is delay and deterrence; the ticket is usually about the door habit, not the steel.

Cameras, motion, and the guard who can ask a question

Afternoon ticket from HR: someone left a backpack in the stairwell and wanted footage "from the vibe of 2-ish."

Facilities pulled CCTV coverage for the stair landing. Retention is finite; angles are honest about what they miss. A motion detection alert on the roof hatch had fired last week and turned out to be a seagull. The alarm system on the server room door still means "stop and call," not "curious peek."

A security guard met us at the landing, because cameras watch and humans challenge.

"I can ask who you are," she said. "The lens cannot."

We did not find a villain. We found a contractor who had signed the visitor log and then gotten lost. Escort gap, not Hollywood. If the hatch alarm is weird after hours, that is a SOC and Facilities hunt — not a help-desk curiosity walk.

Badges, biometrics, and the reader that is not a suggestion

Dev from engineering pinged that his badge worked the lobby but failed the wiring closet.

"I wave it like a magic wand," he said. "Sometimes I badge and hold for a friend. Sometimes I let two people in on one beep because the line is awkward."

"One beep, one person," I said at the badge reader. "Your access badge is a proximity / RFID credential. Convenient. Also cloneable in the wrong hands if we treat it as the only factor. Closet policy is badge plus biometrics on the fingerprint reader — something you have and something you are. Holding the door for a friend is piggybacking with better branding."

Dev practiced the fingerprint until the reader stopped judging him. I put the shared-beep habit in the ticket comments. He said the line would still be awkward tomorrow. I believed him.

Nora from ops dropped a loaner laptop at the desk with a cable dangling.

"Travel kit," she said. "Hotel desk. I stepped away for ice."

"Cable lock next time," Jules said. "Full-disk encryption is necessary and insufficient when the whole chassis walks. Lock the hinge to the furniture."

Clean desk, privacy filter, shoulder surfing

Finance row after lunch looked like a paper museum. Payroll printouts. A sticky with a door PIN. A laptop open to a vendor portal while someone "just grabs coffee."

"Clean desk policy," I said, trying not to sound like a hall monitor. "Sensitive paper goes in the locked drawer or the shred bin. Lock the screen. That PIN sticky is a gift to shoulder surfing."

I handed out two privacy filters for people who sat near the aisle. Narrow viewing angle. Maya installed hers. Sam asked if the filter made him look paranoid. Jules said, "Install it anyway."

USB blockers, Faraday, air gap

Sam appeared with a phone and a story about a free charge kiosk at the airport that had felt hungry.

"Use a USB data blocker," I said. "Power only. No data pins. Public charge ports should not get a conversation with your contacts."

Meanwhile Security dropped an evidence phone into a metallic pouch before it rode to forensics.

"Faraday bag," the analyst said. "Kill the radios so remote wipe and beaconing get a time-out."

In the same breath they reminded me why the ballot-tabulation laptop for a client demo sat on a table with no Ethernet and Wi-Fi disabled hard.

"Air gap," Jules said. "No cable, no Wi-Fi, no just-this-once USB bridge to a helpful desktop. Isolation is a physical decision."

I added a wiki line about protected cable distribution for the sensitive runs in the server room — conduits and monitoring so taps are not a casual afternoon project — and left the deep electrical romance to Facilities.

Secure areas, visitor logs, hot aisles

Late day tour for a vendor who needed to reseat a drive. Reception already had them in the visitor log. Guest badge. Escort was me.

We badged into the secure area. Camera. Fingerprint. The room was loud in a purposeful way — hot aisle / cold aisle layout so humans walk the cold path and the exhaust stays honest. I did not pretend to be a mechanical engineer. I did not prop the door.

Vendor worked. Escort stayed. No unattended strangers among the racks. The ticket closed with visited, logged, escorted, left.

Cipher locks, clones, and the badge that went on vacation

Rita from marketing Slack-panicked: her badge was "somewhere between the food carts and destiny." She was using a colleague's card "just for today" and wanted me to unlock the marketing closet remotely.

"We do not share badges," I said. "We disable hers in the system, print a temporary at reception, and treat the missing proximity card as lost media — because a found badge plus a helpful door is an unauthorized person with manners."

Facilities confirmed the card was deactivated before lunch ended. Rita collected a temp. She was still annoyed that she had to walk to reception. Jules muttered that badge cloning demos exist for a reason and that PIN-on-reader plus biometrics is how we keep a copied card from becoming a copied career.

A separate ticket wanted the old mechanical cipher lock on the storage closet "because numbers feel safer than RFID." I did not dunk on numbers. I said the lock is only as good as rotation and shoulder-surfing resistance — and a code written on the frame is public.

Night walk: eyes, ears, and the sticky that survives

Before close, Facilities invited the desk on a five-minute perimeter walk so we would stop inventing building mythology in tickets. Lot lights were even; one camera looked at the bike rack on purpose; the guard's path crossed the dark corner the seagulls prefer. I noticed how little of this shows up in the queue until something fails — then everyone discovers they always cared about lighting and CCTV.

Back at the cube I added one sentence to the onboarding blurb for interns: physical security is layered. Outer delay, middle detection, inner authentication, human challenge. The help desk sits where politeness tries to short-circuit the stack.

At 5:35 the queue was quieter. The wedge was gone. Rita had a temp badge. The client from the morning had gotten in through reception, late.

Jules clipped her bike lights on. "Tomorrow the queue invents a new way to hold a door open."

I shut the laptop. Somewhere a badge reader beeped for a person who actually belonged. I went out through the lobby glass into the mist.
