# The Rep Who Couldn't Say Yes

*Tamarack Supply • MIS Module 6: Global Information Systems • Hover over highlighted terms for course definitions.*

## The Discount That Used to Be a Phone Call

For eleven years, when Marcy Lund needed a better price on woven wire fencing before spring, she called Dale Kowalchuk. Dale ran U.S. sales for Prairie Wire, a fencing manufacturer headquartered in Saskatoon with a galvanizing plant in Saskatchewan and a newer wire-drawing plant outside Ho Chi Minh City. Dale would listen, complain about steel prices, and approve four percent off a truckload by the end of the call.

This March, Dale listened and said he would have to submit a request.

"Submit it to who?" Marcy asked.

"To Saskatoon. Everything goes to Saskatoon now."

Marcy put him on speaker so Dana Okafor could hear. Prairie Wire had been bought the previous fall by a much larger industrial group, and the new owners were rebuilding how the company ran.

## How Prairie Wire Used to Be Built

Dana had taken one international business course and spent the afternoon reminding herself of it, because Marcy wanted to know whether this was permanent.

Prairie Wire had always been a [[multinational_corporations_mncs|multinational corporation]]: a company with assets and operations outside its home country, selling across borders, managed mostly from headquarters. But companies like that could organize in very different ways, and the way they organized decided who could say yes to Marcy.

The old Prairie Wire had run a [[multinational_structure|multinational structure]]. Production, sales, and marketing were decentralized. Dale's U.S. office set its own prices, ran its own promotions, and used its own order system. Saskatoon handled the money and nothing else. That was why Dale could approve four percent on a phone call.

The new owners were moving to a [[global_structure|global structure]]. Information systems were centralized at headquarters, and subsidiaries had little autonomy. Every price, every order, every system decision went through Saskatoon. Dale still had a title. He no longer had a pricing screen.

"Is there anything in between?" Marcy asked.

There were two in-between shapes, Dana said. An [[international_structure|international structure]] looked like the old multinational one, except the subsidiaries leaned on headquarters for process and production decisions; Dale might have kept pricing but lost any say in which plant filled the order. A [[transnational_structure|transnational structure]] was the one the textbooks admired, where the parent and the subsidiaries designed policies and logistics together. Prairie Wire's new owners had not picked that one.

## The System Behind the Change

The pricing change was only the visible part. Behind it, the new owners were rolling out a [[global_information_system_gis|global information system]] that tied the Saskatoon headquarters, the two plants, and the U.S. and Canadian sales offices into one set of applications and one database, gathering and moving data across borders, currencies, and languages.

For Tamarack, that had an immediate effect. Dale's old order system had accepted Tamarack's purchase orders by email and spreadsheet. The new one accepted only electronic orders in a standard format, and the cutover date was six weeks away. Dana would have to build the connection from Tamarack's purchasing system or Marcy would have to type every order into a supplier web portal by hand.

The other effect was better. Prairie Wire's new [[global_supply_chain_management|global supply chain management]] system showed every order's progress from wire drawing in Vietnam to galvanizing in Saskatchewan to the rail yard in Spokane. For the first time, Marcy could see that her April truckload of field fence was sitting on a container ship, not "in production," and plan around it.

## Walt's Idea

Walt Brandvold's response to losing the four percent was to propose cutting out Prairie Wire entirely.

"Their Vietnam plant makes the wire," he said. "We'll buy from a factory over there ourselves. Private label. Tamarack Ranch Fence."

What Walt was describing was [[offshore_outsourcing|offshore outsourcing]]: choosing a firm in another country to provide the product. Marcy had looked into it before. She brought her old notes to the meeting.

A factory in Vietnam would take Tamarack's order, but at a minimum of four containers per production run, which was more woven wire than Tamarack sold in two years. Lead time was fourteen weeks plus shipping. Tamarack would own quality control, customs, and every damaged roll. And Prairie Wire's galvanizing, which was the reason ranchers trusted the product, was done in Saskatchewan, not Vietnam. A cheaper roll from the same wire-drawing plant would not have the same coating.

Walt asked whether they could partner with the other feed dealers to hit the minimum. Marcy said maybe, and that it would take a year to organize.

## The Data Prairie Wire Wanted

Two weeks into the integration work, Prairie Wire's project team in Saskatoon sent Dana a new requirement. The new owners wanted sell-through data from their dealers: what sold, at which store, to what kind of customer, so they could plan production. They offered better terms to dealers who shared it.

Dana read the data specification twice. It asked for store-level sales by product, which was fine. It also asked for customer ZIP codes and loyalty segments attached to each sale.

She raised it with Ruth Halvorsen. Some of Tamarack's loyalty customers were Canadian — ranchers from British Columbia who crossed the border to shop in Colville — and the loyalty terms had never mentioned sending anyone's information to a foreign company. Prairie Wire's own team admitted that their Vietnam operation had local rules about what kinds of data could be stored and moved there. [[transborder_data_flow_tdf|Transborder data flow]] rules restricted what data could be captured and transmitted across those borders, and nobody on either side had a clear answer about which rules applied to a Colville sale to a Canadian customer, reported to a Saskatoon headquarters, and replicated to a plant in Vietnam.

Ruth decided Tamarack would send store-level totals by product and nothing about customers. That disqualified them from the better terms.

## Where It Landed

Dana built the electronic order connection on time, mostly by working two Saturdays. The first week after cutover, three orders failed because Tamarack's item numbers didn't match Prairie Wire's new global catalog, and Marcy typed them into the portal by hand while making it clear whose fault she thought that was.

The spring truckload arrived at list price. Dale called Marcy from his own phone a month later to say he was leaving Prairie Wire for a smaller competitor in Montana that still let its salespeople set prices, and that he'd be glad to quote her. Marcy asked whether that competitor's galvanizing was as good. Dale said he didn't know yet.

Walt's private-label idea went into a folder labeled "feed co-op," next to the purchasing partnership from the winter, which had not met since.
