# The Video From the Kettle Falls Lot

*Tamarack Supply • MIS Module 4: Personal, Legal, Ethical, and Organizational Issues • Hover over highlighted terms for course definitions.*

## Forty Seconds

The clip was forty seconds long. It showed a man in a Tamarack Supply vest loading feed bags into a pickup at the Kettle Falls store, and then, after a cut, the same truck at a fence line with a cow lying on its side. A caption in block letters said that Tamarack's sweet feed had killed three head of cattle and the company was covering it up.

By Tuesday morning it had eleven thousand shares. By Tuesday noon Walt Brandvold had watched it nine times.

"Is it real?" he asked Teo Vasquez. "Could somebody make that with a computer? One of those AI things?"

Teo had already looked. The loading shot was real: Kettle Falls, last October, from a customer's phone, posted at the time in a thread about the new loading dock. The fence-line shot came from a livestock-health page in Alberta, two years old, about bloat in a dairy herd. Somebody had cut them together in a free editor and added the caption.

"It's not a [[deepfakes|deepfake]]," Teo said. "Nobody generated a fake person or a fake voice. It's a [[cheap_fakes|cheap fake]]. Two real clips and a lie in between."

"Does that matter?"

It mattered for what they could prove. A deepfake would have been an argument about pixels. A cheap fake could be taken apart: here is the original of this shot, here is the original of that one, here are the dates.

## Who Started It and Who Just Shared It

Ruth Halvorsen ran the meeting with a printout of the top comments. Dana Okafor had sorted the accounts that shared the video into two piles.

The first pile was three accounts created in the past month, all of which had posted the clip within ten minutes of each other, and one of which also posted coupon codes for Northland Farm Supply. Whoever ran those had known the clip was false and pushed it anyway. That was [[disinformation|disinformation]]: fake information distributed on purpose. The whole package — the cut, the caption, the made-up cover-up — was [[fake_news|fake news]] in the plain sense, a story built to deceive.

The second pile was everybody else. Neighbors, 4-H parents, a county commissioner's wife. They believed it, or half-believed it, and shared it because they were worried about their animals. What they were spreading was [[misinformation|misinformation]]: false either way, but without intent to mislead.

"So we go after the first pile and we talk to the second," Ruth said. "Gently."

Walt wanted to name Northland. Hank Pruitt, who had been through a defamation scare at a previous company, asked Walt what proof he had that Northland itself was behind three anonymous accounts. Walt had a coupon code. Walt agreed to wait.

## The Clerk Who Reposted It

One of the second-pile accounts belonged to Jesse, the clerk from Colville. He had shared the clip with the comment "not true i work there," which was well meant and had pushed it to six hundred more people.

Tamarack's [[acceptable_use_policies|acceptable use policy]] covered company computers, the network, and company email. It said nothing about employees' personal accounts. Ruth asked HR whether it should, and HR asked where the line was: Jesse defending his employer on his own phone, on his own time, was not misconduct, and a policy that tried to forbid it would be unenforceable and resented.

What Dana could do was train. She put together a short session on [[digital_citizenship|digital citizenship]] for the store staff: using technology safely, ethically, and responsibly, which in this case meant don't amplify a rumor by arguing with it, send it to head office, let one account answer. Jesse sat through it with his arms crossed and said afterward that at least he'd tried to do something.

## What the Web Store Knew

The video drove traffic to the web store. Some of it was angry. Some of it was curious about Tamarack's sweet feed. Marcy Lund noticed the spike and asked whether they could email everyone who'd looked at the sweet-feed page to reassure them.

Dana walked her through what the site actually knew. The site set [[cookies|cookies]], small text files with an ID tag stored in each visitor's browser, so it could recognize a returning visitor. The web server kept [[log_files|log files]] of every page request. Between them, Tamarack could tell that a given browser had visited the sweet-feed page six times. It could tell the email address only if that browser belonged to someone who had logged in.

"Then we email the ones who logged in," Marcy said.

Some of them had opted out of marketing. Sending to them anyway would be [[spam|spam]] in the legal sense and in the sense that mattered: unsolicited email, sent because Tamarack wanted something. Marcy cut the list to people who had opted in. It shrank from 1,400 to 310.

## The Woman in Bavaria

Two days into the mess, a request came through the web store's contact form from a customer in Germany. She bred Haflinger horses, had ordered a specialty harness from Tamarack two years earlier, and had seen the video on a horse forum. She wanted to know every piece of personal data Tamarack held on her, and then she wanted it deleted.

Hank asked whether a German law applied to a store in Spokane Valley. Dana had already looked it up. The [[general_data_protection_regulation_gdpr|General Data Protection Regulation]] protected the personal data of people in the European Union, and Tamarack had sold to her, shipped to her, and kept her address. It applied enough that their lawyer told them to comply.

Answering her took Dana most of a day: the order record, the shipping address, the cookie ID tied to her login, the log file entries, an abandoned cart from last spring. Deleting it raised a second problem. Her harness was still under warranty, and the only proof of purchase was the order record. Dana wrote back, explained the tradeoff, and asked whether she wanted the warranty record kept. She did not reply.

## The Domain

On Friday, a customer called to ask why the "official Tamarack site" was selling sweet feed at forty percent off. The site was tamaracksupplyco.com. Tamarack's was tamaracksupply.com. Somebody had registered the near-match in March, copied the logo and product photos, and set up a checkout page.

That was [[cybersquatting|cybersquatting]]: using a domain name to profit from someone else's trademark. The logo and photos were Tamarack's [[intellectual_property|intellectual property]], which gave the lawyer something to work with. Even so, the dispute process would take weeks, the registrar was overseas, and every day the site stayed up it was collecting card numbers from Tamarack customers.

Hank asked whether they could buy the domain. The registrant wanted eight thousand dollars. Walt said he would not pay a thief, and the lawyer filed the complaint.

## Who Gets to Say What on the Page

The comment section on Tamarack's own social page was the last fight. Teo wanted to turn comments off until it blew over. Ruth didn't.

Her argument was partly about [[social_media_networking_ethics|social media networking ethics]]: the page was supposed to be open and fair to everyone using it, and a company that shut down comments the moment they got critical would look like it had something to hide. Dana added a [[business_networking_ethics|business networking]] angle — the page was also how the stores talked to their regulars, and the regulars hadn't done anything wrong. They kept comments open, deleted the three disinformation accounts' posts as coordinated fake accounts under the platform's rules, and left the angry neighbors alone.

Ruth also named the [[accountability|accountability]] question nobody wanted. If one Tamarack customer's cattle actually did get sick this spring, from anything, the clip would be the first thing anyone remembered. The company had a responsibility to have its feed tested and the results public before that happened, not after. She ordered the tests.

## The People Who Never Saw the Correction

The feed tests came back clean the following week. Tamarack posted the results, the two original clips with dates, and a short video of the Kettle Falls manager explaining what had happened. Most of the shares stopped.

Ruth's last point was the one that didn't resolve. About a third of Tamarack's ranch customers didn't use social media much and had slow or no home internet; the [[digital_divide|digital divide]] ran straight through the territory. Many of them had heard about the video secondhand, at the sale barn or church, and would never see the correction online. Ruth had the stores print the test results and post them at the counter. Sweet-feed sales at Kettle Falls were still down eighteen percent a month later.

Teo, meanwhile, had a pallet of old register terminals from the lane upgrade sitting in the warehouse. With the week's attention on doing things in public, Ruth asked him to handle them under [[green_computing|green computing]] practice: wipe them, send them to a certified recycler, and keep the certificate. The recycler charged more than the landfill would have. Teo filed the certificate in the same folder as the GDPR request, which was still open.
