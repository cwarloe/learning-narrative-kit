# The Closet Gets Too Hot

*Tamarack Feed & Supply Co. • MIS Module 10: Emerging Trends, Technologies, and Applications • Hover over highlighted terms for course definitions.*

In the second week of July, the window air conditioner in the feed-room closet quit during a heat wave, and by two in the afternoon the thermometer Teo Vasquez had taped to the rack read ninety-seven degrees. The new NAS shut itself down to protect its disks. The billing program, the shared drives, and the time clock went with it.

Teo got a portable cooler from the DC and had everything back up by four. Then he went to Ruth Halvorsen's office and said what he'd been saying since the spring: Tamarack shouldn't be running its own servers in a closet.

Ruth agreed, mostly. The ERP selection had started, and every vendor on the shortlist was asking where Tamarack wanted the system to run. She gave Teo two weeks to bring her a recommendation with costs, and sent Dana Okafor with him to the farm-retail technology expo in Boise, because two of the ERP vendors would be there and she wanted someone in the room who would ask about the stores.

Dana wrote her question at the top of a notepad before they left: what happens at Republic when the line goes down? The Republic store sat at the end of a rural internet connection that failed a few times every winter. Arlo had run the store through every outage on paper tickets and a calculator, and Arlo had retired in June.

## The Vendor With One Answer

The ERP vendor Teo liked best had a booth with chairs, which at an expo meant they expected a long conversation. Their sales engineer, Brenda, opened by asking how many registers Tamarack ran.

Her company sold its ERP one way, as software as a service. The vendor ran the application in its own data centers, and Tamarack's people would use it through a browser for a fee. Her slides said Software as a Service and her price sheet said on-demand software, and both meant a subscription, monthly or annual, instead of a license Tamarack would own.

"Can we buy a license and run it ourselves?" Teo asked.

"Not anymore. We started out as one of the application service providers, hosting other people's software. Now we only host our own."

Teo tried the layer below. He could rent servers, storage, and networking, which was infrastructure as a service, and run the ERP on them himself, minus the closet. Brenda said her license didn't allow it. That ended the idea for the ERP. It didn't end it for the propane billing program, which nobody sold as a service because nobody else used it. That could move to rented infrastructure until the MSP rewrote it, and the MSP would want platform as a service for the rewrite, so its developers could build in a ready-made environment without managing servers either.

Every banner in the hall said cloud computing, and Brenda's offer was the whole of it: software, storage, and computing power from one provider over the Internet, with the applications in a browser and the data on the provider's servers. Dana asked her question. Every sale at Republic would cross that rural line to reach those servers. What happened when it was down?

Brenda didn't pretend the answer was nothing. "We're a public cloud. If the store can't reach the Internet, it can't reach us."

The fix was edge computing: a small server in the store's back room, so that the processing and the data sat at the near edge of the network, next to the registers. The registers would talk to it. It would talk to the cloud when it could, and hold the sales when it couldn't.

"What about the banner?" Teo asked. The banner advertised distributed cloud computing, which meant the vendor placed pieces of its cloud services in data centers closer to customers so the round trip was shorter. The nearest one was Seattle. That would make head office faster. It did nothing for a store whose line was cut, because Seattle was still on the far side of the cut. Dana wrote *edge, not distributed* under her question.

Teo drew what Tamarack would own on the back of the price sheet. The ERP would live in the vendor's public cloud. The edge servers in the eleven stores would be a private cloud, services and hardware on a private network that Tamarack ran. One of each made a hybrid cloud.

"Eleven boxes in back rooms is a private cloud?" Dana said.

"They run our services on our network. That's all the term asks."

"It's also eleven more things that can overheat."

"The back rooms have doors," Teo said.

On the way out, Dana counted the clouds Tamarack was already in. The web store was on one provider, the loyalty program on another, and Counter Assistant on a third. Tamarack had been multicloud since spring without deciding to be: three public clouds, three contracts, three support numbers, and nobody who had compared them.

* * *

That night Teo forwarded the quote to Hank Pruitt, because Hank would ask anyway. Hank called the hotel with the sheet open in front of him.

```table
caption: The quote Teo forwarded to Hank
| Line | What Tamarack gets | Billed |
|---|---|---|
| ERP subscription | The ERP, through a browser, run by the vendor | Per user, per month |
| Edge server, per store | A back-room server that keeps registers running offline | Per store, per month |
| Backup as a service | Offsite copies of files, folders, or whole drives | Per gigabyte, per month |
| Cloud storage | Shared drives on space leased from the provider | Per gigabyte, per month |
| Data as a service | Storage, integration, processing, and analytics for reporting | Per month |
| Security as a service | Monitoring and threat response, run by the vendor | Per user, per month |
```

Hank read every line out loud and stopped on the last column each time. He asked what they paid for the closet now. Teo said nothing, except when it failed.

"That's my point," Hank said. "It fails once a year and costs nothing the other eleven months. This costs something every month, forever."

Dana went down the sheet with him. The backup line was the one she wanted most; the next failed disk would not mean a day of rewinding tapes nobody had tested, which was where Tamarack had been in the winter. Cloud storage would move the shared drives off the NAS that had just shut itself down. The data line was the merchandising data mart from the spring, rented instead of built, and Marcy already had the one she'd built. Dana crossed it off. Hank would have signed the security line at midnight, after the payment fraud, and Dana wrote *after the ERP* beside it.

"Is there anything where we pay for what we use?" Hank asked.

Teo had one: the propane route optimizer from the spring trial, which ran once a week and needed a bigger machine than Tamarack owned for about an hour. Buying that server meant paying for six idle days. Utility computing meant renting the power for the hour and paying for it like heat. One booth had wanted to run the optimizer on grid computing, pooling many computers for problems too big for any one of them to finish in time. Teo said a single rented server did it in forty minutes, and the booth had stopped returning his questions.

In the morning Hank said yes to backups and asked for everything else in writing.

## Walt Drives Down

Walt Brandvold drove down for the second day with an errand. Arlo's replacement at Republic had called him: the store's biggest cattle account, a ranch that bought feed by the truckload, wanted proof of what its cattle had eaten, because its packer was paying more for beef that could be traced. The cattle association had a way to provide it, at a booth in the second hall.

The association was building a blockchain for beef. It was a decentralized network that recorded each transaction (calf born, vaccinated, fed, shipped) as a block of data that couldn't be altered once written. Dealers would record every feed lot they sold to a member ranch. The association ran it on blockchain as a service, renting the cloud infrastructure for its own blockchain from a third party instead of building it.

"Where do I sign?" Walt said.

Dana asked where Tamarack's records would be written.

"From the store, at the sale," the rep said. "The chain records transactions across connected devices. Each dealer's system is one of the devices."

"And if the store isn't connected?"

"Then the record waits."

The one store that needed the blockchain was the store most likely to be offline when a truckload went out. The edge server would hold the sale, but the rep didn't know whether the association would accept a record written six hours late. She said she would find out.

While they waited she showed them the rest of the program. Members could be paid in cryptocurrency, which Hank had already refused once when a rancher asked. Feed credits were fungible tokens on the chain, any one as good as another. The "Founders' Herd" collectibles were non-fungible tokens, one unique painted cow apiece.

"Those are ugly," Walt said.

"The sale-day passes are better," the rep said. They were semi-fungible tokens, interchangeable as admission until the sale and one of a kind afterward. Walt asked again whether he could sign today.

"You can sign anything," Dana said. "The first record that matters gets written at Republic in January."

Walt took the brochure and walked the rest of the hall with her, and after an hour he was asking her question himself.

At the forklift maker's booth he put on a virtual reality headset and drove a computer-generated forklift through a DC that felt real enough that he braked with his foot. The headset was an egocentric environment, total immersion. The cheaper version ran on a laptop as an exocentric environment, a window view the trainee could watch but not reach into.

"Does it need the Internet?" Walt asked.

It didn't. Dana took pictures for Eli. Down the aisle, a university was showing a CAVE, a cube-shaped room with rear-projection screens for walls, for designing grain elevators in true 3D. Walt asked what it cost and laughed.

The fencing company's augmented reality app laid a virtual fence line over a pasture seen through a phone's camera and counted the posts. Next year's version was mixed reality, where a virtual gate was anchored to a real post and swung shut against it.

"Does it work with no signal?" Walt asked.

"It downloads the pasture map first."

"Half my customers' pastures don't have signal," Walt said, and the rep wrote it down.

A feed brand had a virtual world where customers met as avatars on a simulated farm and could shop for feed, and called it its first step into the metaverse. Dana remembered Ruth's point from the week of the video: about a third of the ranch customers had slow internet at home or none. Walt didn't stop.

He did stop for 3D printing. A parts supplier was building a replacement gear for a discontinued grain cart one thin layer at a time from a digital model, and ranchers brought that problem to the counter every week. Walt took the card. The research booth next to it had 4D printing, printed valves made of materials that changed shape on their own when the soil dried, and a wire maker said its nanotechnology coating, engineered at a molecular scale, would double the life of galvanized wire. Dana wrote down the claim and not the number.

Two booths she walked past. One was demonstrating contextual computing, a loyalty app that sensed a customer walking in, knew her purchases from last spring, and suggested a refill; Dana thought of the GDPR request still open in Teo's folder. The other sold implanted microchips, rice-sized chips placed between the thumb and forefinger to hold an ID or a credit card. Walt said Tamarack already sold microchips, for horses.

The afternoon keynote was on quantum computing, computers built on qubits for problems ordinary ones couldn't solve. Walt left at the first equation.

The cheapest thing they saw was a QR code, a square of black modules on white, printed on a feed-bag tag. Scanning it opened the feeding guide.

"That works at Republic," Walt said.

"The code does. The guide is a web page."

So the two lines that mattered, the daily ration and the warning, would be printed on the tag beside the code. That meant a bigger tag.

The association rep found them at the exit. The system would accept late records, she said, but anything written more than a day after the sale was flagged for review, and flagged records didn't qualify for the packer's premium.

* * *

Back in Spokane Valley, Teo's recommendation to Ruth was the hybrid: the ERP as a service in a public cloud, an edge server in each store, backups to a backup service, the billing program on rented infrastructure until it was rewritten, and nothing in the closet but network equipment. Tamarack would also join the dealers' community cloud, built for the exclusive use of farm-supply dealers with the same compliance problems, for pesticide sales records only. Two of the dealers on its member list were the ones Marcy had wanted to form a buying group with in the winter.

Ruth approved it. Walt did not sign with the cattle association. He said he would look again after the Republic edge server had been through a winter, and he called the ranch himself to tell them.

The ranch moved its show-cattle feed to a dealer in Omak that had signed, and kept buying range cubes from Republic. The QR codes went on the feed tags in September. In October the Republic store's internet went down for six hours during the first snow, and its edge server, installed only two weeks earlier, kept the registers running until the line came back. Then it took eleven hours to sync, and two loyalty customers got double points.
