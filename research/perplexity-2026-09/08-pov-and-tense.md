## Headline

Almost no empirical work directly compares POV or tense for **learning outcomes in
instructional fiction**. Nearest evidence is narrative persuasion, general reading
comprehension, and educational texts. On that base plus craft convention, the answer
recommends **first-person past-tense** narration from the junior technician, with explicit
signaling via headings and narrator retrospection, and a senior colleague carrying expert
exposition.

## Full answer

### Empirical work on POV/tense and learning

No study manipulates first vs third person, or past vs present tense, in business/technical
instructional fiction for adults and then measures comprehension, retention, or transfer.
(Research: absence of evidence.)

Closest adjacent evidence:

- A 2026 Frontiers study with upper-primary English learners compared first-person vs
  third-person omniscient narration. Third-person omniscient yielded a modest overall
  advantage in composite reading performance; first-person scored higher on inferential
  processing and micro-level comprehension; third-person on structural coherence and
  macro-level comprehension.
- A film-based study on preservice teachers compared first- vs third-person narrative
  films and found higher short- and long-term memory scores for the third-person version,
  even though first-person increased empathy and perspective taking.
- Work on visualisation and perspective suggests recalling narratives in first person can
  improve memory, but that concerns recall perspective, not the text's grammatical POV.

For tense: a Collabra paper experimentally compared present vs past tense short stories and
found **no differences** in self-reported mental simulation, transportation, or
appreciation. Past-tense versions were read slightly **more slowly** word-by-word, but this
did not show up in comprehension or simulation measures. A separate experiment found
present tense increased conceptual identification with the character's viewpoint, but did
not link this to learning outcomes.

Narrative vs expository comparisons show narrative formats generally produce better recall
and often equal or better comprehension, but these analyses do not break down effects by
POV or tense.

**Bottom line:** no direct comparison exists for instructional fiction. POV shifts *what*
aspects of comprehension are strongest (local/inferential vs global/coherence). Tense
appears to have negligible impact on comprehension or transportation.

### Narrative transportation, POV, and tense

Does POV affect transportation? Mixed, leaning first-person:

- An online and lab study (N≈600) found first-person stories evoked more narrative
  engagement than third-person, moderated by affective alexithymia.
- A Radboud study found first-person pronouns produced higher immersion and emotional
  engagement, though attention, mental imagery, and reading times did not differ.
- A 2022 meta-analysis of 16 health-promotion experiments found first-person POV produced
  slightly higher identification and perceived susceptibility, with effects **stronger when
  stories were written in past tense** and when the protagonist resembled recipients.
- Other work finds small or nonsignificant differences by POV.

Does tense affect transportation? The Collabra study measured transportation explicitly and
reported **no effect** of present vs past. No study was found showing a robust
transportation advantage for present tense in English narrative. (Research: explicit gap.)

### Is transportation linked to learning, or mainly persuasion?

- Green & Brock's seminal work shows transportation increases story-consistent beliefs and
  favorable protagonist evaluations — attitudes and evaluations, **not knowledge tests**.
- Later narrative-persuasion work uses risk perceptions, attitudes, and behavioral
  intentions as outcomes, not domain knowledge or skill acquisition.
- A 2024 study comparing narrative vs non-narrative texts found narrative versions rated
  more engaging, higher on an immersion index, marginally better recall — but comprehension
  scores were similar, and transportation was not analysed as a mediator of learning.
- Storytelling narrated videos beat lecture-style on retention and transfer, but without
  formal transportation scales.

**Strong** evidence transportation predicts belief and attitude change. **Weak, indirect**
evidence that immersive narrative slightly improves recall. It is **not empirically
justified** to assume increasing transportation improves comprehension or transfer in adult
technical training.

### Signaling, narration, and cognitive load

Mayer's signaling principle: people learn better when cues highlight the organization of
essential material. Empirical signaling work focuses on **typographical and structural
cues** — headings, outlines, arrows, highlighting, stressed words in speech.

- Mautone & Mayer: adding outline, headings, arrows, and stressed key words to a narrated
  animation improved problem-solving transfer (effect size ≈ .74).
- Lorch's text-signaling work examines headings, previews, typographical emphasis.

**No study was found that treats retrospective narrator commentary ("that was the
conversation that changed everything") as a signaling device and tests its impact on
learning.** (Research: explicit gap.) Existing signaling research is agnostic about tense.

The idea that past-tense narration has a native signaling mechanism is **plausible but
untested — a design inference, not a research finding.** Nothing indicates signals must be
encoded via tense rather than layout or wording.

### Present-tense narration and processing cost

The Collabra eye-tracking study reports the **opposite** of the markedness hypothesis:
past-tense stories elicited slightly **longer** first-fixation and gaze durations per word
than present-tense, and the difference was not tied to simulation, transportation, or
appreciation.

A separate experiment found present tense increased the likelihood readers adopted the
character's visual point of view, with the main effect on perspective taking, not
processing cost.

**No study concludes present-tense narration is measurably harder to process than past
tense in English.** Craft essays calling present tense "marked" or "fatiguing" are
qualitative judgments, not empirically backed processing data.

### Epistemic constraints of first-person novice narrators

- Third-person omniscient vs objective voice experiments show omniscient narration makes
  readers more likely to adopt an internal character perspective; objective voice nudges
  toward an external observer.
- First-person tends to provide "character-bound" information requiring more local
  inference; third-person omniscient supports broader coherence building.

**No empirical instructional-design literature directly addresses a first-person narrator
who is a novice yet must present substantial expert content.** (Research: explicit gap.)

Craft practice in business fables resolves this by:

- A first-person viewpoint character whose expertise grows over time, with **mentors
  providing explanations through dialogue** (*The Phoenix Project*).
- Or third-person past-tense omniscient with a central viewpoint character (*The Five
  Dysfunctions of a Team*), letting the narrator supply expert commentary freely.

These are conventions, not experimentally validated solutions.

### Person and tense in key instructional novels

Verbatim opening lines were not reproduced, citing copyright. Descriptions from accessible
excerpts:

- **The Goal (Goldratt, 1984)** — first-person narrator (Alex Rogo), mixing **present
  narration for on-scene action** ("Peach asks … I can't tell him …") with past tense for
  backstory ("when he tried to call me at home"). Conversational first person, largely
  tracking events as they unfold.
- **The Phoenix Project (Kim et al.)** — unambiguously **first-person present tense**. Bill
  Palmer narrates driving, answering his phone, dealing with outages as they occur.
- **The Five Dysfunctions of a Team (Lencioni)** — the fable section is **third-person past
  tense**.

### Recommendation given

**POV: first-person limited from the junior technician.** First-person tends to increase
engagement, identification, and immersion, particularly when the protagonist resembles the
reader. Mirrors *The Phoenix Project*. The epistemic constraint is handled structurally:
the narrator stays a novice and **expert content is delivered through mentor dialogue**,
not implausibly expert internal monologue.

**Tense: predominantly simple past for narration, present in dialogue.** Because present
tense is not empirically more vivid, past can be chosen freely to gain the narrative
affordance of retrospective signaling. Aligns with the register of postmortems and incident
reports that IT professionals already read.

**Signaling:** use typographical and structural signaling as the *primary* mechanism
(scene titles tied to objectives, marked decision-point terms, diagrams). Treat narrator
retrospection as *secondary* semantic signaling.

**Managing expert content:** let the narrator misunderstand or oversimplify a term on first
appearance, then have the mentor correct or deepen it in dialogue, tying vocabulary to
concrete stakes. Occasionally step back with short retrospective mini-cases to borrow
third-person's coherence advantage while staying in first person. Consider clearly-marked
expository sidebars or end-of-chapter playbooks to consolidate procedures, segmenting
narrative from schema-building.

## Limits

What this answer does **not** establish, and what it corrects:

1. **No direct evidence exists for the core question.** Every POV/tense recommendation here
   is extrapolation from adjacent literature plus craft convention. The answer is explicit
   about this and should not be cited as though it settled the matter.
2. **The signaling argument for past tense is untested.** It was this kit's main stated
   reason for preferring past tense. The answer labels it a design inference. Retain past
   tense on craft-convention grounds, not on Mayer.
3. **The "present tense costs more to process" claim is contradicted.** Eye-tracking found
   *past* tense slightly slower per word, with no comprehension difference either way.
   Any contract language asserting a processing cost for present tense was wrong.
4. **Transportation does not buy learning.** It is robustly tied to persuasion and only
   weakly and indirectly to recall. Designing for immersion alone is not justified.
5. **The POV evidence is genuinely mixed, not one-directional.** Third-person omniscient
   showed a modest advantage on composite reading performance and macro-level coherence,
   and better memory scores in the film study. First-person wins on engagement and
   identification. The recommendation weighs engagement higher; that weighting is a
   judgment call, not a finding.
