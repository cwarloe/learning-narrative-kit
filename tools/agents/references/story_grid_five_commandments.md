## Framework from the supplied article

- **Purpose and scale (pp. 1–2):** Story structure communicates a controlling idea
  through genre-related changes in value. The article applies structure across
  scales, from small units to a full story. Establish the unit being assessed;
  do not confuse a paragraph boundary with a complete scene.
- **Inciting incident (pp. 3–4):** An event upsets the protagonist's balance,
  positively or negatively, prompting a goal. Classify it as **causal** (a
  character's active choice) or **coincidental** (an unexpected or accidental
  event). Identify the event, disturbance, and resulting goal separately.
- **Turning Point Progressive Complication (p. 4):** Attempts to regain balance
  fail and complicate the situation. The final turning point makes the initial
  strategy no longer viable. It comes through **action** or **revelation**.
  Show which strategy fails and why the turning point grows out of the same problem.
- **Crisis (pp. 4–5):** The failed strategy forces a binary choice between
  incompatible alternatives with meaningful stakes. Classify it as a
  **Best Bad Choice** (both alternatives impose an undesirable loss) or
  **Irreconcilable Goods** (both protect something valued, but cannot both be
  retained). The two alternatives need not be spoken aloud, but both must be supported by the text; the reviewer must not
  invent a dilemma that the character never faces.
- **Climax (p. 5):** The character enacts the answer to the crisis. That action
  under pressure reveals character. Deliberate refusal or silence can enact a
  choice, as the examples show (pp. 6–7); distinguish that from mere inactivity.
  A stated intention or a mentor supplying the solution is not evidence that the
  viewpoint character made the choice.
- **Resolution (p. 5):** Show what results from the climactic choice. Consequences
  must be meaningful in relation to the stakes already established. Identify
  what changed, who bears the cost, and the response to the initiating disturbance.

## Causal checks stressed by the article (pp. 8–9)

1. The inciting incident should promise the climactic action; the climax should
   answer that incident and show how the protagonist has changed.
2. An initiating problem left unaddressed at the end of the reviewed story is a
   structural concern. Distinguish that from a resolved choice leaving costs or
   consequences that later scenes must inherit.
3. Complications should expose the breakdown of the protagonist's strategy.
   An unrelated new event that merely blocks progress is not equivalent.
4. The turning point must force the particular crisis and its available options.
5. Track the same protagonist through the turning point, crisis, and climax.
   Do not stitch one person's dilemma to another person's decisive action.
6. The resolution must pay off the crisis stakes. A consequence-free outcome
   invalidates stakes the scene has presented as unavoidable.

## Boundaries and examples

The article's examples cover both a whole story and individual scenes. Its
Hamilton example places the resolution at the beginning of the next song
(pp. 7–8). Review enough adjacent text to check such a continuation before
calling a resolution absent; explicitly state when that continuation lies
outside the requested scope. A separate lab cannot supply a missing story ending.

A review may infer a possible controlling idea from the value change and its
cause, but must label the inference. It cannot establish the author's intended
genre, canonical theme, or reader response on its own. The article's claims about
reader impact are its claims, not evidence that a learning unit has been tested.

## Applying it alongside this repository's rules

The Story Grid checks and the repository's requirements should be reported
separately when they differ. Repository precedence still governs revisions.
An intentional fragment may be appropriate; a complete scene missing a supported
crisis should still be identified as such, even when it teaches a useful concept.
Do not claim it meets all five criteria merely because it is a short vignette.
Conversely, do not require five explicit speeches or mechanical story beats in
every paragraph, or enlarge a realistic workplace incident just to add drama.

Portland Desk stories may show the answer. Labs use worked examples and transfer
problems, not withheld endings. A consequence that carries into the next chapter
is compatible with a resolution if the climactic choice and its immediate result
are actually shown. Any deliberate departure from the article should be labeled
as an author decision rather than silently treated as compliance.
