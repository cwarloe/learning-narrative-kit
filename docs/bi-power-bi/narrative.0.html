By September, Ruth Halvorsen opened five workbooks every Monday: Davenport's KPI, the propane tank summary, the store packets, the DC's damage log, and Hank Pruitt's cash report. Each one was good. Together, they took her most of Monday morning, and every store manager wanted their own copy of at least two of them.

Eli Mendez had started as day-shift supervisor two weeks earlier. The last unit of his course was an introduction to Power BI. Ruth asked whether it could turn five workbooks into one thing she could share. Eli said he'd find out, and spent three evenings finding out.

Power BI was a platform for analyzing and visualizing data from different sources and formats. Eli built the report in Power BI Desktop on his laptop. It could reuse the Power Query steps and the data model from the Excel workbooks, so the tank readings, sales, and store tables came in without being rebuilt.

Each chart, map, or table on a page was a visual. The course used visualization for the same thing; both meant a chart, graph, or table showing relationships in the data. Eli built four pages: Company, Davenport, Propane, and DC. Davenport got the KPI card and the customer value map. Propane got a line chart of tank levels by route and a table of tanks forecast to fall below 20 percent this week. The DC page got damage by carrier.

The filters were where Eli spent the most time, because the same filter could be set at three different scopes, and setting it at the wrong one was easy.

A report-level filter applied to every page of the report. Eli set one for the current fiscal year so no page would accidentally show last year's numbers.

A page-level filter applied to everything on one page. On the Propane page, he filtered Product Category to Propane, so every visual on that page showed only propane.

A visual-level filter applied to one visual. On the Company page, the "Top customers" bar chart had a filter of its own for the top 10 by sales, so it didn't affect the store totals next to it.

His first draft had the propane filter at the report level by mistake. Every page showed only propane, and the DC page was empty. Ruth spotted it in the preview and asked whether the DC had stopped receiving feed.

Ruth's favorite feature was the one she found herself. The company sales chart showed sales by store. Eli turned on drill mode for it, and when Ruth clicked a store's column, the chart went one level deeper, showing that store's sales by month. Clicking a month showed weeks. An arrow took her back up.

She drilled into Davenport and found the week in August when sales had jumped, which turned out to be the week a competitor in Ritzville had closed for a remodel.

To share the report, Eli published it from Desktop to the Power BI service, Microsoft's cloud-based platform for publishing reports, datasets, and dashboards to share with colleagues. Ruth could open it in a browser. The data could refresh on a schedule there, so nobody had to press anything on Monday morning.

Then the store managers tried to open it. Most of them couldn't.

Sharing reports in the Power BI service required paid licenses for the people viewing them, on Tamarack's plan. Ruth had one. Eli had a trial. The twelve store managers, Hank, Marcy Lund, and the dispatcher had none. Hank priced it out for everyone and came back with a monthly number he said he would not be signing.

Ruth bought licenses for five people: herself, Hank, Marcy, the dispatcher, and Eli. The store managers would get a PDF of their store's page emailed every Monday morning, generated by a scheduled subscription. They could see it but not drill into it.

Ruth opened one report the next Monday instead of five, and her Monday morning took forty minutes. Hank's cash page refreshed correctly. The propane page caught two tanks below 15 percent that the dispatcher had missed.

Curtis at Colville replied to the first emailed PDF to ask why he couldn't click on anything. Ruth wrote back that it was a budget decision. Curtis said that head office could drill into his store and he couldn't, and that he'd noticed.

Eli's course ended the following week. His final grade was a B+. He printed the certificate and pinned it to the wall of the day supervisor's office, next to the shift schedule.
