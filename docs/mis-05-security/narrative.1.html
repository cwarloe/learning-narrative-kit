"And if it's in my email," Hank said, "whoever's reading my email has it too."

"Right. So the portal uses two keys." With asymmetric encryption, each side had a public key that anyone could have and a private key that only its owner held. A message locked with Tamarack's public key could be opened only with Tamarack's private key, so anyone who intercepted it got nothing they could read.

"Who hands out the keys? If I get a public key in an email from 'Karen,' I'm right back where I started."

That was the question PKI answered. The key pairs came from a trusted authority and were shared through it, so Tamarack never had to trust a key just because of who claimed to have sent it. The signature worked the same pairing in the other direction: the seed company signed a change with its private key, and Tamarack checked it with the public key from the authority. Only the holder of that private key could have produced it.

"So if a signed change comes through and the money goes wrong," Hank said, "Karen can't tell me she never sent it."

"That's the idea." The signed form bound both companies to what it said, which was what the course called nonrepudiation.

"And if somebody steals her private key?"

Teo admitted that then they'd be back to the phone call. Hank put last year's paper invoice in his top drawer, with Karen's number on it.

The web store already used TLS for checkout. The MSP pointed out that one old supplier integration still negotiated the older SSL protocol, and turned it off; the supplier's integration broke for two days.

Store managers who worked from home would connect through a VPN, a secure tunnel to the office network. The office firewall would block everything else coming in. An intrusion detection system would watch traffic for attack patterns and alert Teo. Teo said the alerts would go to a shared mailbox this time, not a person who'd left.

Some items didn't make the cut. The MSP had pitched a zero trust design, where every device and person had to prove they were secure on every connection, inside the building or out. Teo wanted it. It meant replacing the register network, and Walt deferred it to next year.

The DC already used fingerprint readers on the time clock, a form of biometric security. The vendor's sales rep pitched extending it to the office, and then went further, describing zero login systems that would recognize users by voice and typing pattern, DNA identification that built an "eDNA" behavioral profile, and a brain password that read brain activity through a special hat. Walt said he would wear a hat to log in the day the seed company paid back his fourteen thousand dollars.

The feed-room closet got a lock with a keypad, the cheapest of the physical security measures on the list. The billing box got wiped, rebuilt, and moved to the new NAS-backed setup from the spring. That brought up fault-tolerant systems again, and from there Ruth asked for the thing she had been meaning to ask for since the server failed: a written business continuity plan covering how Tamarack would keep selling feed and propane if head office's systems went down for a week. Dana was assigned to draft it. Nobody gave her a deadline, which she took to mean she should set her own.

The bank's fraud desk called Hank back in April. The receiving account had been emptied the day after the February deposit. The money was gone.

The web store went down once more that spring, for forty minutes on a Saturday, from a denial-of-service attack that flooded it with requests. Nobody ever knew whether it was connected. The MSP put the store behind a filtering service and added it to the monthly bill.

Hank used the token every morning and said he hated it every morning. In June the seed company's real accounts-receivable office received an email, from an address with one letter doubled, asking whether Tamarack's bank had changed. Karen forwarded it to Hank with one line: "Is this you?" Hank called her back at the number on the paper invoice.
