It had been raining on the east glass since before I sat down. Jules had already claimed the whiteboard. A bike helmet hung off her monitor arm, and the sticky under which decision? now said which factor?

"Cards in the drawer are for ports," she said when I reached for a blank one. "Someone will swear two passwords are multifactor. Talk the habit into a decision."

The first ticket in the help desk queue was already yelling in all caps.

Nora and the policy that keeps changing the locks

Nora in finance could not reset. She had tried her old favorite three times. The portal said no.

"I always rotate the season and the year," she said on the phone. "Spring2024!, Summer2024! — that used to work everywhere. Now it hates me. Also it wants sixteen characters. Who remembers sixteen?"

I pulled the account. Password age was ninety-one days. Policy max was ninety. Her last change had slid under the wire last quarter; today the portal was enforcing password expiration before it would talk about a reset path.

"Expiration is why I'm calling," Nora said. "Every ninety days I invent a worse password and write it on a sticky. My cousin's home lab uses a passphrase — four words, no sticky. Is that allowed, or do we still need the symbol salad?"

Jules leaned in just enough to be heard. "Ask what we are optimizing for."

"Length and uniqueness," I said. "Complexity rules still exist here — upper, lower, digit, symbol — but a long passphrase that clears length usually beats a short spicy one. And no password reuse across work and the streaming account you use at lunch. That Spring2024 pattern is reuse with a costume."

Nora snorted. "Costume. Fine. What do I type?"

We walked a passphrase she would actually remember, not a movie quote she had posted on socials. I reminded her the company password manager would store a unique password for every app so she was not inventing seasons. She groaned about another vault, then admitted her browser had already offered to save three variants of the same secret.

She set the passphrase. She did not install the manager on the call. "I'll do the vault later," she said. "I have a close." I put manager still pending in the ticket and left it open.

Ken and the MFA that is not MFA

Ken in sales was locked out of the CRM after a phone wipe. He wanted a quick fix.

"I already have multifactor," Ken said. "Password plus a PIN on the laptop. Two things. That is MFA, right? My old shop said so."

I felt the flashcard reflex and almost agreed because the sentence had two nouns.

Jules's sticky was visible from my chair.

"Identification is you typing your username," I said. "Authentication is proving it. MFA means two different factor types. Password is something you know. PIN is also something you know. Same bucket. That is single-factor with extra typing — not multifactor authentication."

Ken was quiet for a second. "So what counts as the second thing?"

"Something you have — phone app, hardware token, security key. Or something you are — fingerprint, face. Location can be somewhere you are, a context signal, not a substitute for a second factor when policy asks for MFA."

"Password plus PIN was a house rule, then," he muttered. "Okay. Enroll me for real."

Soft tokens, SMS, and the Approve reflex

The enrollment ticket for Ken turned into a hallway class for two other people who "just need the code thing."

I started with the authenticator app. The soft path is a soft token on the phone that shows TOTP codes — six digits, thirty seconds, clock-bound. We scanned the QR at his desk, not at the coffee bar. I mentioned shoulder surfing once; he angled the screen.

"What about texts?" Ken asked. "SMS is easier. I do not want another app."

"SMS OTP is still a second factor," I said, "but it is the weaker possession story — SIM swap, intercepted texts. Policy allows it as a backup, not as the primary. App first."

He installed the app. We generated backup codes and he put them in the password manager, not in a Notes file titled codes. Push was next: the IdP offered push notification approve/deny.

Jules walked past with a granola bar. "Tell him about fatigue before he trains his thumb."

"MFA fatigue," I said. "If you get a push you did not start, tap Deny and call us. Attackers spam Approve until someone gets bored."

Ken nodded like a man who had tapped through terms of service. "Deny if I did not ask."

Across the aisle, Rita from marketing wanted the same enrollment but "without the phone, I lose phones." We issued a loaner hard token for her travel week and scheduled a security key once procurement finished arguing about USB-C.

Lockout, biometrics, and the laptop that thinks it knows a face

Mid-morning: Dev from engineering, account lockout after a script retried a stale secret forty times. He was embarrassed and late for a stand-up.

"I pointed a job at the old service password," Dev said. "It hammered. Now I cannot even get in to fix the hammer."

"Lockout is doing its job," I said. "Failed authentication budget spent. I unlock after we rotate the secret in the vault, not before — otherwise the script just spends the budget again."

He waited on the timer. He did not like waiting. The script stayed stopped until the secret lived in the vault.

While Dev waited, Lila from HR stopped by with a laptop lid.

"Windows Hello says my face," she said. "Is that real biometrics, or theater? Also can I turn off MFA if the laptop unlocks me?"

"Biometrics are something you are," I said. "Fine for device unlock. Company apps still want MFA at the identity provider. Unlocking the lid is not the same as proving you to every SaaS tile."

She kept the app. She looked like someone who had hoped for a shorter answer.

Offboarding leftovers and the admin sticky

Afternoon started with HR pinging offboarding for a contractor who had left Friday. Badge returned. Laptop wiped. Access had not followed.

Jordan from IT ops shared a screen. The contractor still had a license on the design tool, a shared mailbox membership, and — Jules made a noise — a line in a wiki with a shared admin account password last rotated "sometime before the rebrand."

"That is not a password," Jules said. "That is a campfire story."

We treated it as a deprovisioning failure. Disable the cloud login. Pull group memberships. Kill the API token someone had minted "temporarily" in March. For the shared admin: move the secret into vaulting, rotate it, and stop pretending six people can share one identity and still have accounting that means anything.

"Provisioning was fine on day one," Jordan said. "We created the account, put them in the contractor role, shipped the laptop. Day last is where we get sloppy."

I opened a follow-up ticket to map the contractor role in RBAC so the next person inherited a bundle we could revoke in one motion — not a scavenger hunt across five admin consoles. Jules, still in the thread: "Least privilege on the way in. Same muscle on the way out."

Privileged shortcuts and break-glass myths

Network dropped into the same thread. Jared — still proud of last week's SSH rewrite — wanted a standing domain admin for "closet emergencies."

"I have a Pi at home that is basically god mode," he said. "At work I still type a normal user password and wait. If the IdP hiccups, how do we get in?"

"Checkout, not a lifestyle," Jules said. "I've got a vendor call in ten. Walk him through PAM and then get off the thread."

We walked the desk-level PAM story without turning it into a product pitch: vault the break-glass path, do not laminate it. Break-glass exists for when MFA and IdP paths are actually down — sealed, monitored, painful on purpose. Day to day, Jared requests just-in-time elevation for the change window, does the work, loses the rights when the window closes. Authorization for admin actions should not be a permanent personality trait on his account. Privileged access is a checkout.

Jared sighed. "Fine. I will stop naming shared accounts after the street food cart." He did not get the standing admin. He opened a change ticket for the next window instead.

SSO ticket: the app that wants its own password

Late afternoon. Vendor portal for facilities. Title: need password for EnergyDash. Description: IT please create a login.

I called. Dana — same truck as the FTP week — was between buildings.

"The portal has a Sign in with company button," Dana said. "Also a Register. Register asked me for a new password. I made one. Now Finance says that is wrong."

"SSO," I said. "We do not want a snowflake password in EnergyDash if we can help it. Our IdP already authenticated you this morning. The portal should trust a federation assertion, not a new local account."

"They said SAML on the implementation call," Dana offered. "Or OAuth. They used both words like synonyms."

Jules, still on her vendor call, muted long enough to say, "Desk translation, Ethan," and unmuted again.

"Federation means their app trusts our IdP. SAML is the enterprise assertion pattern you will see on the wiki checklist. OAuth shows up when an app wants a token to call an API without eating your password — related paperwork, different shape. For this ticket, click Sign in with company, refuse Register, and if Register is the only path, we escalate — local passwords in vendor tools become reuse magnets and offboarding blind spots."

Dana tapped something. "Sign in with company worked. It bounced me through the usual MFA push. I denied the first one because I forgot I had started it. Then I approved the second."

"Good," I said. "Keep the local register button unemployed."

Passwordless rumor and the end of the shift

Last ping was not a fire. It was a rumor. Someone in Slack asked when we would go passwordless so they could throw their password manager into the river.

Jules typed one line between vendor questions: security keys and device-bound passkeys are on the roadmap; throwing secrets into the river is not a migration plan.

I added the desk version for the person who would still call: passwordless replaces the knowledge factor with possession and biometrics wired through the IdP — it does not mean no proof. Until then, unique passwords in the manager, MFA that is actually multifactor, and no PIN cosplay.
