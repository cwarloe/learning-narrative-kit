# Portland Desk, Chapters 00–2: Adversarial Review

**Scope.** This review covers three chapters. "The Four Rubber Bands" (`docs/portland-desk/narrative/00-the-four-rubber-bands.md`, Chapter 00) takes place in an East Portland kitchen and a Beaverton testing center. "The Paper Cert" (`examples/a-plus-hardware-helpdesk/narrative.md`, Chapter 1) takes place at the Gresham warehouse. "The Live Board" (`examples/a-plus-os-helpdesk/narrative.md`, Chapter 2) takes place at the Portland ROC. All three are identical to `/workspace/sorkin-desk/portland-desk/narrative/00`, `01`, and `02`. I read all three in full. Chapter 00 is treated as a full chapter, not a prologue to be forgiven. This is a story-craft review only. Technical accuracy was handled in the 2026-10-22 nitpick pass and is not reopened here.

## Verdict

The book opens with a miracle and then keeps performing it: Ethan goes from a fifty-eight on a practice test to a comfortable pass in thirty-six hours because his father gave one speech and he bought four rubber bands, and the next two chapters keep handing him the answer just as cleanly. Chapter 2 is the closest to working because Jules keeps taking the wheel away from him. Chapter 00, which should teach the reader that this book makes people pay, teaches the opposite on its first pages.

## What to protect

- **The eighty-three dollars and fourteen cents.** It is the best stake in the three chapters, because it is specific and it is his money. The voucher ate his grocery budget. Keep it, and then collect on it (see problem 1).
- **The fifty-eight percent and the cards on the linoleum.** Chapter 00 opens with Ethan failing, alone, at night. That is the right first image for a book about a novice.
- **The father refuses to say everything will be fine.** "He did not offer an easy compliment or tell me that everything would turn out fine." That is a person helping, not magic. The problem is what the speech is allowed to accomplish afterward, not that he gives it.
- **Ken blocking the cabinet latch with his clipboard.** "Don't guess, Ethan." That is the best line in Chapter 1, and it is exactly the kind of help Charles is asking for.
- **The second failure in Chapter 1.** The pin is straightened, Ethan expects victory, and the screen gets worse, with fourteen thousand framing faults per second. That is a real reversal.
- **The dead phone signal.** "The signal in the high-bay metal building was weak." Someone on the team already closed the internet-rescue door. Lock it.
- **Jules's mute button.** She cuts Ethan off mid-promise to re-image Maya's machine, and she attaches a cost: two hours, certificates, and Maya's shortcuts. That is the model for the whole book.
- **Chapters 1 and 2 stop on a question.** The instinct is right. Chapter 00 is the one chapter that ends on a happy drive into the rain, and it is the one that most needed a question.
- **Canon.** Harrowmere is named, the Gresham warehouse comes first, the Portland ROC desk under Jules comes second, and Ethan reaches the ROC by internal referral, not by relocating. He lives in East Portland and drives to Beaverton only to sit an exam. No canon drift in the chapter bodies. (See problem 9 for continuity snags.)

## Problems, ranked by cost

### 1. Chapter 00: Ethan fixes his whole study method in one night and passes comfortably.

This is the most expensive problem in the book, because it sets the rules for everything that follows. On Tuesday night Ethan scores fifty-eight percent. His father delivers a single speech about McMaster-Carr catalogs. Ethan sorts three hundred cards into four rubber-banded stacks in one sitting, "finally turned off the buzzing fluorescent fixture," and on Thursday morning he scores 742 against a 675 cut. Nothing went wrong between those two scores. He did not sleep badly, did not misread a question, did not take a second practice test that came back sixty-four. The reader learns on page one that in this book, insight is instant and insight is enough. That is precisely the pattern Charles is complaining about in the later chapters, and Chapter 00 is where it is born.

**Fix.** Make the rubber bands a partial fix with a price. He sorts the cards that night, retakes the practice test at one in the morning, and scores sixty-six, still under the line. He goes into Beaverton not knowing whether the method works. Then either he passes narrowly, with a score close enough to the cut that he cannot pretend it was calm (680 instead of 742), or he fails one of the two exams and has to find another voucher's worth of money out of the eighty-three dollars. Either way, the method should be something he is still testing in Chapter 1, not something already proven.

### 2. Chapter 00: The exam asks exactly the questions his new system was built to answer.

"When the practical scenario questions appeared on the screen, my green, yellow, and blue diagnostic frameworks took over completely." The scenarios that appear are an APIPA address and three long beeps for unseated memory, which line up with the green and yellow bands as neatly as if the exam had read his notebook. The red-band trivia also cooperates: he recognizes it, answers it from the cram, and "did not panic." This is the convenient search result from Charles's note, moved into a testing booth. The world is supplying the answer key.

**Fix.** Give him at least one scenario that does not fit any of his bands, and make him guess. Make one red-band item something he cut from the cram on purpose, and let him feel the cost of that choice in the booth. Let the clammy hands do something: he changes an answer he had right. The reader should leave the testing center believing the method helped, not that the method is a cheat code.

### 3. Chapter 00: The father is an oracle who appears, solves the chapter, and leaves.

The father walks in, pours water, gives a perfectly shaped lesson that conveniently produces exactly four categories, and "padded back down the carpeted hallway." He never returns. He never sees the cards, never asks how the test went, never turns out to be wrong about anything. That makes him a delivery mechanism for the four-band thesis, not a person. Charles asked for help that comes from people rather than magic, and a person who works like magic doesn't count.

**Fix.** Let the father's advice be incomplete or partly wrong, and let Ethan push back and be right about something. Ethan is already set up to argue ("A computer isn't like a milling machine, Dad"). Give that objection one beat of victory: the father concedes that some of the red-band trivia is just memorization, and he does not know how to help with it. Then bring him back once, on Thursday night or Friday morning, to ask about the score, so that the relationship continues past the lesson. If he does not come back, the reader should feel the absence, and it should matter in Chapter 1, where his tools and his pencil appear.

### 4. Chapter 00 ends happily ever after, and it spends the lesson Chapter 1 needs.

The chapter closes with an embossed score report on the passenger seat and Ethan pulling into traffic with "the physical proof that I had earned my license to step through the help desk door." The narrator even pre-concedes the next chapter's thesis: "The paper did not magically make me a senior engineer." Then Chapter 1 opens by telling us "I believed that passing those multiple-choice tests made me a real technician," and spends its first beat shattering an illusion that Chapter 00 already told us he did not hold. The setup undercuts the payoff. Chapter 1's title, "The Paper Cert," wants the reader to have watched Ethan overvalue the paper. Chapter 00 instead shows him being modest about it.

**Fix.** End Chapter 00 on the unresolved thread, not the victory lap. Cut the modest closing line. Let him be proud, a little too proud, of the score report, and end on something that has not been fixed: the money, the job, or the fact that nobody on the dock cares. He drives back to Gresham for the night shift, and Ken does not look up from the clipboard. Now Chapter 1's shattered illusion is an illusion the reader actually watched him build.

### 5. Chapter 2 resolves Chapter 1's cliffhanger offstage, as a total win, in a subordinate clause.

Chapter 1 ends at 4:20 a.m. with Seattle forty minutes from canceling and the clock divider unknown. Chapter 2's first paragraph then tells us that Ken's letter certifies "that I had diagnosed and restored the main sorting line under live dispatch pressure." So he made it, cleanly, and the night cost nothing. Every bit of tension Chapter 1 built is refunded at the door of Chapter 2. Combined with problem 1, the reader now has two data points that say chapters in this book end in wins, whatever the page says.

**Fix.** Let the night cost something and carry the bill forward. Ethan fixes the baud rate at 5:11, and Seattle rolls, but Boise does not. Or the line comes back, but the Kapton-taped ribbon cable is a field patch that fails again in week two, and the maintenance tech who has to replace it properly is not grateful. Ken's letter can still exist, but make it honest: "He got it back up. He was late. He didn't quit." Jules should read that letter, and she should notice the word "late."

### 6. Chapter 1: Ethan arrives with a fully stocked competence kit that nothing has earned.

Chapter 1 says Ethan's flashcard knowledge is an illusion. Two minutes later, he walks to a workbench where his father's toolbox happens to be waiting, pulls out a calibrated multimeter and Kapton tape, measures three rails to two decimal places, identifies the fourth conductor of a forty-conductor ribbon as the culprit, and fixes a bent RS-232 pin with a drafting pencil whose sleeve "measured exactly 0.5 millimeters across." Chapter 00 made this worse, not better. It gave the father a kitchen speech about anatomy, but it never once showed him teaching Ethan to hold a meter. The hands-on skill in Chapter 1 has no history on the page.

**Fix.** Use Chapter 00 to plant one physical skill, and only one. While the father is in the kitchen, he can mention, or Ethan can remember, being made to check a motor with a meter as a kid. Then in Chapter 1, that one skill works, and everything after it is harder. He finds the short only after three wrong places, and in one of them he pulls a connector he should not have. The pencil trick should be his father's trick that he half remembers, and the first attempt should nearly snap the pin.

### 7. Chapter 1 has no second human who knows anything.

Ken supervises, counts minutes, and makes threats. Nobody else on a graveyard warehouse floor at 2:24 a.m. knows the scale, the sorter, or the vendor. The track bible says expert content arrives through mentor dialogue. Chapter 1 has no mentor, so the expertise arrives through Ethan's hands alone.

**Fix.** Add a maintenance lead or a scale vendor on a crackling phone call. Give that person one useful sentence and one confident wrong one: "That scale was reset to factory last month; it's probably at 9600." He is wrong, because someone changed it and did not write it down. Ethan has to choose between seniority and the evidence on his screen, and the wrong advice costs him ten minutes.

### 8. Chapter 2 hands Ethan Safe Mode and BitLocker as if he has done them a hundred times, and Maya is a deadline rather than a person.

Jules sets one rule: "walk me through your diagnostic tree before anyone touches a setting." Ethan immediately reboots a payroll supervisor's machine into Safe Mode without walking Jules through anything. When BitLocker throws a recovery screen, he says, "Don't worry, Maya. That's just BitLocker," and reads her the key in one clean pass. Maya volunteers the key clue about the overnight update unprompted, and her voice is "softening as I asked deliberate questions." Safe Mode works on the first try. That is a string of free wins, and the only rule Jules set is broken without consequence.

**Fix.** Make the BitLocker screen land on Ethan. For a few seconds he has no idea where the recovery key lives, and Jules does not rescue him; she asks, "Where would the company keep that?" Afterward, she marks him down for changing Maya's boot state before walking her through his tree. Make Maya wrong about something important, such as swearing nothing changed overnight, so that the update clue comes out late and grudgingly.

### 9. Continuity snags and drift risks across the three chapters.

These are not canon drift in the chapter bodies, but they will bother careful readers.

- Chapter 00 says he is twenty in July. Chapter 1, in late winter, says he is twenty-one. That works only if his birthday falls in between. The course README says twenty. Write the birthday into the bible once.
- Chapter 00 says he unloads "eighty-pound bags of mortar." The Portland author bible (`docs/research/PORTLAND_AUTHOR_BIBLE.md`, Episode 00 card) says "50-pound bags of concrete mix," and the course README agrees. This is the chapter drifting from the bible, so the fix belongs in the Chapter 00 text.
- Chapter 1 calls the display both "amber monochrome monitor" and "the amber CRT monitor."
- Chapter 2 says "fourteen months on the concrete docks." Chapter 00 implies he has been there at least two months by July. Count it once and write it down.
- The Willamette River plant is in the Portland author bible as a Harrowmere site (Episode 04 and Leah Moreno), so it is not drift. Nothing in Chapters 00–2 uses it. Pending Charles's confirmation, treat it as a site Ethan visits, not a place he works.

### 10. Jules talks like the exam guide, and so does the narrator in Chapter 00.

Jules says, "You walk the CompTIA troubleshooting methodology, and you start by forming a theory you can actually test." No desk lead has ever said that out loud. Chapter 00 has the same problem in Ethan's narration: the blue-band paragraph recites the six-step framework in order, and the green-band paragraph reads like a glossary. The track bible calls Jules a conversational coach, and it asks the narrator to stay a novice.

**Fix.** Let Jules teach through stories and questions, and let the methodology name arrive later in Ethan's narration, as the thing he realizes she was describing. In Chapter 00, let the band descriptions be messy and half right, written by a tired twenty-year-old at midnight, and let Chapter 1 or Jules correct one of them. That is the complication-and-pivot rule the bible already asks for.

## How Chapter 00 sets up, and undercuts, Chapter 1

Chapter 00 does some useful setup. It establishes the money, the father, the machinist's habit of asking what moves and what stays still, and the Gresham warehouse. Chapter 1's multimeter, the father's toolbox, and the drafting pencil all depend on it.

It undercuts Chapter 1 in three ways. First, it spends Chapter 1's thesis, that paper does not make you a technician, in its own closing paragraph, so Chapter 1's "illusion shattered" has nothing to shatter. Second, it teaches the reader that insight equals victory, so when Chapter 1's cliffhanger quietly resolves as a total win in Chapter 2, the reader is not surprised, because the book has never once let Ethan lose. Third, it establishes a blue-band troubleshooting reflex ("A technician never changes three settings at once out of nervous panic"), and then Chapter 1 opens with Ethan power-cycling the terminal three times out of nervous panic. That contradiction is actually a gift, because it shows the method failing under pressure. Right now no one notices it. Ken should notice it, or Ethan should, out loud, and it should embarrass him.

## Revision moves

1. **Make Chapter 00's turnaround partial and expensive.** The sorted cards produce a second practice score that is still failing. He goes into Beaverton unsure, passes narrowly or fails one exam, and the money is not fine. The rubber bands become a method he is still testing, not a proven trick.
2. **End Chapter 00 on an open thread, and let Chapter 1 collect it.** Cut the modest closing line. Let him overvalue the score report, drive straight to a night shift where nobody cares, and walk into Chapter 1 believing the paper is worth more than it is. Then have Ken use the three power cycles against him.
3. **Rewrite the first paragraph of Chapter 2 so Chapter 1's night has a cost.** Ethan restores the line late, at least one trailer misses, and the Kapton patch is a known temporary fix. Ken's letter mentions the delay, and Jules asks about it.
4. **Put a second knowledgeable person in Chapters 00 and 1, and let each be partly wrong.** The father concedes that he cannot help with the trivia and returns once after the test. The maintenance lead or scale vendor in Chapter 1 gives one right fact and one confident wrong one.
5. **Make Chapter 2's BitLocker screen Ethan's problem, and have Jules mark him for breaking her rule.** Ethan carries that mark into Chapter 3, and Maya gives up the update clue late and reluctantly.

## Closing

The team has good instincts: a broke kid with eighty-three dollars, a father who will not lie to him, a dead phone, a worse second failure, and a mentor who grabs the mute button. Now they need the nerve to let those choices cost something, starting on page one. A competent hero is fine. A hero who has never paid for anything is a brochure. Make Ethan barely pass, make him late, make him wrong in front of Jules, and make him walk into each chapter still carrying something from the last one. Readers will follow a junior tech who keeps getting knocked down much further than one who keeps getting lucky.
